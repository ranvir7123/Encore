Read this before you quote a number

We ran the control that could kill our own headline. It killed part of it.

The interesting claim was that a trained model learned when customers have money. So we built random_in_horizon: a policy that picks its retry hour uniformly at random from the identical candidate set the model ranks, starting from the identical cooldown-aware hour. The only difference between them is whether the ranking is a model or a coin.

On held-out data the coin wins by 46%. It is not brute force — it recovered more using fewer attempts. A heuristic with no model at all (fixed_spread10, retry at T+3, T+6, T+9) gets most of the way there too, so most of our headline was available with no learning in it.

  • Holds Beats the industry-standard T+1/T+2/T+3 schedule 2.85x, with 0 compliance violations across all 18 regime-by-policy cells.
  • Does not hold That the model learned when customers have money. On the held-out regime a uniform draw over the same hours beats it.

Our first explanation was wrong too. We blamed a hardcoded near-payday flag in the feature vector, removed it, and retrained. The de-biased model gained 3% and still lost. The real cause is below, and it is a measurement problem rather than a modelling one.

The measurement · regime r1_shifted, held out from training

The model is not ignoring payday. It is two days early.

In this regime, success is not a gradient — it is a step. Before day 25 a retry recovers almost nothing; from day 25 it recovers essentially everything, because salary has landed and the balance clears. Both policies face the same step. The only thing that matters is how many of a capped three retries per customer land on the far side of it.

The model puts 546 retries on days 21–24, immediately before the cliff, and only 151 past it. The uniform control lands 284 past it. That gap is the whole result. A confident near-miss spends a capped budget on days that are reliably empty; a uniform draw at least samples the far side.

Disclosed limitation. The sharpness of this step is a simulator artifact. Portfolio.debit succeeds deterministically once the balance clears, so post-payday success is exactly 100% and pre-payday is near zero. Real balances do not behave like this. The direction of the finding is sound; the magnitude is flattered by the simulator.
